\section{Performance Evaluation}
\label{sec:performance}
%=======================================================================

We evaluate SENTINEL with the simulator released with this paper\footnote{\url{https://github.com/tanzimulalam/Quantum_SAGIN_AoII_Updated_Simulation}, tag \texttt{v2.3.3-manuscript}; the script \texttt{scripts/reproduce\_all.sh} regenerates every table and figure.}
under the parameters of Table~\ref{tab:simulation_parameters}, which are the values used for every figure. Each
configuration runs for 60{,}000 slots of 10~ms, ten minutes of network time, on 30 independent seeds; the policies of
one seed share its topology, source trajectories, fading, and disruption realization, so all comparisons are paired.
We report medians with interquartile ranges, because a few seeds contain long non-line-of-sight passes that dominate
means; means with 95\% confidence intervals, the raw per-seed results, and the paired test tables are in the
repository.

\textbf{Baselines.} \emph{Random} transmits with probability 0.1 per slot over a uniformly chosen visible node with
equal bandwidth sharing. \emph{AoII threshold} is a state-aware policy that transmits whenever its AoII is positive,
at most once per 5 slots, on the node with the best nominal rate. \emph{Whittle index} is the restless-bandit
scheduler of the AoII literature~\cite{Whittle1988, Kadota2018}: the index of each UE's (source state, estimate, age)
arm is computed numerically per source by relative value iteration on a grid of transmission prices, and every slot
the mismatched UEs are ranked by index and admitted at their best node while the node's shared bandwidth still
delivers every admitted packet; it has the threshold policy's observations and rate cap. \emph{PPO} is an actor-critic
agent~\cite{Schulman2017} that selects, once per orchestration interval, a serving node and an update-period level for
every UE, with invisible or failed nodes masked and equal bandwidth sharing, rewarded with the negative mean AoII of
the interval; it is trained for 5000 episodes per network size on 30 seeds disjoint from the evaluation seeds and
evaluated deterministically on the same episodes as every other policy. It acts once per interval with equal
sharing, like SENTINEL, so that the comparison isolates the decision rule from the actuation; a learned per-slot
policy that observes the source would be a different baseline, and that role is filled by the threshold and
Whittle policies. \emph{Nominal SENTINEL} runs Algorithm~1
with the nominal scenario alone ($L=1$). \emph{SENTINEL} is the robust schedule with the five-scenario set. \emph{SENTINEL
+ trigger} uses SENTINEL's association and bandwidth with transmissions triggered by the observed mismatch,
rate-capped at the chosen period. Every policy receives the same acknowledgement feedback and applies the same
link-failure rule of Section~\ref{sec:feedback}. The exact reference for Section~\ref{sec:exact_validation} is the integer linear program
$\mathscr{P}_3$ solved with HiGHS~\cite{HiGHS2018}.

for every policy, set by handovers and non-line-of-sight passes rather than by the scheduler.

\subsection{The Certificate and the Value of Robustness}
\label{sec:certificate}
%-----------------------------------------------------------------------

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.58\columnwidth]{figures/certificate_vs_realized.pdf}
\caption{Certified worst-case AoII versus realized average AoII per orchestration run, robust and nominal SENTINEL.}
\label{fig:certificate}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:certificate} plots, for every run, the time-weighted certified worst case against the realized
average AoII; the per-epoch statistic is reported in the text. The realized average stayed within the certificate in
98.7\% of orchestration epochs for the robust schedule and 98.5\% for the nominal one; the violations are passes that
enter the non-line-of-sight state, whose 17 to 25~dB clutter loss exceeds the 12~dB deep-shadowing scenario. The certificate is valid against the scenario set it was computed from, and it is
conservative: the certified worst case exceeds the realized average by a factor of 12 to 20 (median 15.5 over runs),
because every scenario applies its outage to every inter-delivery interval of the epoch, which amounts to a
persistent disruption, whereas realized disruptions are intermittent. Per epoch the certified-to-realized ratio has median 17 (IQR 13 to 23) over 3975
epochs, and the 1.3\% of violating epochs are not marginal: a blocked pass pushes realized AoII to 1.2 to 2.1 times
the certificate at the median and up to 5.4 times, so the certificate is a guarantee against the scenario set, not a
prediction of typical behaviour. The certificate is proved for the
periodic schedule, whose deliveries do not depend on the source state; the triggered variant transmits on observed
mismatch, which the renewal argument of Section~\ref{sec:renewal} does not cover, so we check it empirically: its
realized average stayed within the certificate of its schedule in 97.9 to 98.7\% of epochs.

What the scenario set buys is visible in the certificate, not in the realized AoII. Across the 30 paired seeds the
robust schedule's certified worst case is 2 to 7\% below the nominal schedule's (paired-median ratio 0.93 at 5 UEs to
0.98 at 20 UEs), lower in 67 to 100\% of seeds, with Wilcoxon signed-rank $p\le 9\times10^{-5}$ at every size after
Holm correction. The realized AoII of the two is statistically indistinguishable at every size ($p\ge0.08$), although
the robust mean at 20 UEs is lower, 0.39 against 0.46, because the robust schedule keeps 21 to 27\% of deliveries on
base stations. The gap between SENTINEL with the trigger and the threshold policy, 0.02 to 0.03 in AoII, is small but
significant at every size ($p\le0.013$), and PPO is significantly behind SENTINEL from 10 UEs on.

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.7\columnwidth]{figures/noack_ue_sweep_average_aoii_median.pdf}
\caption{Median average AoII without acknowledgement feedback or link-failure handling (60 seeds).}
\label{fig:noack}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Without failure feedback.} Whether the network can react to a dead link matters more than any scheduling
choice. Figure~\ref{fig:noack} repeats the sweep with the link-failure rule disabled for every policy, on 60 seeds
because the distributions become heavy-tailed: median AoII rises by an order of magnitude for all policies, the
certificate holds in 87 to 92\% of epochs, and the threshold and Whittle-index policies, which coincide here as well
and concentrate 98\% of their traffic on the best nominal satellite, tie the SENTINEL schedules at 5 and 10 UEs and
fall 25 to 30\% behind SENTINEL with the trigger at 15 and 20 UEs (Hodges-Lehmann differences $-0.77$ and $-0.71$,
Holm-corrected $p=0.023$ and $0.029$ against the threshold policy and $0.034$ and $0.043$ against the Whittle policy),
while the SENTINEL schedules keep 18 to 27\% of deliveries on base stations. Robust and nominal SENTINEL remain
indistinguishable in realized AoII here as well ($p\ge0.41$). The measurable product of the robust scenario set is
thus its lower certificate, obtained in both regimes, and the measurable value of a scheduled base-station share
appears when feedback is absent.

\textbf{Scenario-set size.} With the list extended by a mild 6~dB shadowing scenario and truncated to
$L=1,\ldots,6$ at 10 UEs, realized AoII is flat in $L$ (0.265 to 0.278), and the certificate and the base-station
hedge move only once the handover of the second shared satellite enters the set (certified worst case 4.57 to 4.28,
satellite share 0.79 to 0.74 between $L=4$ and $L=5$). The mild scenario never binds: removing it leaves every
schedule's certificate unchanged on all 30 seeds at every size, which is why the paper's set has five members.

\subsection{Topology, the Whittle Index, and Capacity}
\label{sec:topology}
%-----------------------------------------------------------------------

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.66\columnwidth]{figures/topology_average_aoii_median.pdf}
\caption{Median average AoII at 10 UEs by deployment (log scale).}
\label{fig:topology}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:topology} compares deployments at 10 UEs. Two base stations cover only the UEs within 5 to 7~km of the
$20\times20$~km$^2$ area, so ground-only monitoring fails for every policy (median AoII 63 to 81). Two satellite
serving slots alone bring the per-slot policies to 0.14 and the SENTINEL schedules to 0.18 to 0.28, and the
integrated $2+2$ and $3+3$ deployments are the best for every policy, with SENTINEL routing 26 to 36\% of its
deliveries to base stations. The one deployment in which the SENTINEL schedules are clearly worse than the per-slot
policies is a single base station with a single satellite slot (medians 1.6 to 1.9 against 0.87 to 0.90, and 5.4 for
the triggered hybrid): with one slot every handover is a total outage for every UE on it, and a scheduled policy
loses more during the recovery than one that retransmits the moment the link returns; this is a limitation of
scheduled transmission at that topology.

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.62\columnwidth]{figures/capacity_average_aoii.pdf}
\caption{Median average AoII of the Whittle-index and AoII-threshold policies at 20 UEs versus bandwidth per node.}
\label{fig:capacity}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Whittle index and capacity.} At the bandwidths of Table~\ref{tab:simulation_parameters} a node delivers 19 to
40 simultaneous equal sharers within one slot, so capacity never binds and the Whittle scheduler makes the same
decisions as the threshold policy (identical AoII to three decimals at every size and topology, $p\ge0.18$); the
threshold baseline of this paper is the Whittle-index policy of this regime. Figure~\ref{fig:capacity} reduces the
bandwidth per node at 20 UEs: at 5~MHz the threshold policy is 57\% worse than the index scheduler (0.239 against
0.152, $p=4\times10^{-9}$), and at 2~MHz its simultaneous transmissions collapse every shared link (median AoII 33.5,
delivery ratio 0.16) while the capacity-aware scheduler stays at 0.16; SENTINEL's dedicated bandwidth levels avoid
this regime by construction. Its one weakness is the ground-only deployment (81 against 63), where its admission rule
never attempts a node that cannot deliver at the nominal rate.

\subsection{Quantum Resources and Device Results}
\label{sec:device}
%-----------------------------------------------------------------------

Table~\ref{tab:hardware} reports the sampling of the hardware-sized domains on the \texttt{ibm\_kingston} and
\texttt{ibm\_fez} processors (Heron r2, 156 qubits; $\varkappa=4$, depth 1, 4096 shots per domain, transpiled at
optimization level 3 with eight routing trials; 27 October to 1 November 2026).
Every scenario weight, ADMM target, and QAOA angle was optimized on the simulator and each device job executed only
the final sampling circuit of one or two domains; the 38-qubit domain exceeds the statevector simulator and received
the angles of the largest simulated domain of its run, relying on parameter concentration~\cite{Brandao2018}. Per run
the table lists the feasible-shot fraction, the probability of the optimum among the feasible shots, and the same
probability under uniform sampling of the run's feasible one-hot set, which we enumerate; the product of the first
two is the probability that a single shot is an optimal assignment. The energy spread of the feasible set is listed because several hardware-sized domains are nearly flat: where it is below 2\%, any
feasible assignment is within 2\% of the optimum and the sampler only has to achieve feasibility; on the other domains
it is 35 to 307\%, and uniform sampling reaches an approximation ratio of only 0.62 to 0.91.

\input{table_hardware}

Three facts follow from the table. First, from the greedy warm start, which is itself optimal on every hardware-sized
domain, the device returned an optimal assignment in 32 to 85\% of all shots on the 6-UE domains, 91 to 99\% of the
feasible shots, against 1.6 to 25\% for uniform feasible sampling. This measures the retention of an already-optimal
warm start under noise, not a search, and we state it that way. Second, at 38 qubits the per-shot probability of the
optimum, 0.6\%, equals the uniform baseline: noise destroys 97\% of the shots, and only among the surviving feasible
shots does the warm start still show (24\% optimal against 0.6\%). The best feasible sample equalled the exact optimum
of its domain in every run and every decoded schedule attained the ILP optimum of its instance, but at 38 qubits this
is what 4096 uniform feasible samples would also achieve. Third, from the $W$ state, with no warm start, run on the same processor as the greedy start, the device is a uniform sampler of the
feasible set: the probability of the optimum among feasible shots is 0.03 to 0.33 against the uniform 0.06 to 0.25 of
the same run (each run re-solves the scenario weights, and on the two seed-2 domains this changes the optimal set, so
the table lists the baseline per run), between 0.52 and 2.04 times the uniform value on every 6-UE domain, and the
approximation ratio of feasible shots exceeds the uniform value by at most 0.07; at 38 qubits no feasible shot was optimal. The 1.3-fold concentration of one
noiseless layer is lost to hardware noise. Every decoded schedule still attained the ILP optimum of its instance
(at 38 qubits the best domain sample was suboptimal but tied at the network worst case), because the feasible sets
are small and the decoder keeps the best of thousands of shots.

The feasible-shot fraction measures the noise: 59 to 86\% at 8 to 18 qubits with up to 142 two-qubit gates, 35 to 43\%
at 20 to 24 qubits with about 300 gates, and 2.6\% at 38 qubits with 606 gates, bracketed by the FakeTorino and FakeFez noise
models (52/33\% and 70/48\% on the 18- and 20-qubit domains of the first instance); the $W$-state circuits, 13 to 113 gates deeper for the state preparation, give the same
fractions on the same processor (34 to 74\% and 3.5\% on \texttt{ibm\_kingston}).
A few hundred more two-qubit gates would leave fewer than one feasible shot in a thousand, which places the present
limit of the quantum step at about ten UEs per domain at depth 1. Error suppression does not move it: re-sampling the
24- and 38-qubit domains in one calibration session with dynamical decoupling (XY4)~\cite{Viola1999}, and with
dynamical decoupling plus Pauli twirling~\cite{Wallman2016}, lowered the feasible fraction from the in-session control
values of 25.0\% and 2.4\% to 21.1\% and 0.7\%, and to 13.8\% and 0.2\%, because these circuits have almost no idle time
in which decoupling could help and twirling inserts further gates. Because the unmitigated yield of one circuit varied by ten
points between calibration sessions (34.8\% against 25.0\%), all device comparisons are made within one session.

We therefore claim no quantum advantage: in simulation the QAOA layers only concentrate a constrained sampler (0.42
at depth 2 from the $W$ state), and on every hardware-sized domain greedy association is already optimal. The instances on which it is not, 50 UEs and above in Table~\ref{tab:optimality_gap},
need 140 to 200 qubits per domain, beyond simulation and beyond the gate budget that the feasible-shot fractions
above allow on current devices; whether a sampler at that size concentrates on the optimal set is the question this
work leaves open.
