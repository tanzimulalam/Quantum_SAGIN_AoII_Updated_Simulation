\section{Performance Evaluation}
\label{sec:performance}
%=======================================================================

We evaluate SENTINEL with the simulator released with this paper\footnote{\url{https://github.com/tanzimulalam/Quantum_SAGIN_AoII_Updated_Simulation}, tag \texttt{v2.0-manuscript}; the script \texttt{scripts/reproduce\_all.sh} regenerates every table and figure.}
under the parameters of Table~\ref{tab:simulation_parameters}, which are the values used for every figure. Each
configuration runs for 60{,}000 slots of 10~ms, ten minutes of network time, on 30 independent seeds; the policies of
one seed share its topology, source trajectories, fading, and disruption realization, so all comparisons are paired.
We report medians with interquartile ranges, because a few seeds contain long non-line-of-sight passes that dominate
means; means with 95\% confidence intervals, the raw per-seed results, and the paired test tables are in the
repository.

\textbf{Baselines.} \emph{Random} transmits with probability 0.1 per slot over a uniformly chosen visible node with
equal bandwidth sharing. \emph{AoII threshold} is a state-aware policy that transmits whenever its AoII is positive,
at most once per 5 slots, on the node with the best nominal rate. \emph{Whittle index} is the restless-bandit
scheduler of the AoII literature~\cite{Whittle1988, Kadota2018}: the index of each UE's (source state, estimate, age)
arm is computed numerically per source by relative value iteration on a grid of transmission prices, and every slot
the mismatched UEs are ranked by index and admitted at their best node while the node's shared bandwidth still
delivers every admitted packet; it has the threshold policy's observations and rate cap. \emph{PPO} is an actor-critic
agent~\cite{Schulman2017} that selects, once per orchestration interval, a serving node and an update-period level for
every UE, with invisible or failed nodes masked and equal bandwidth sharing, rewarded with the negative mean AoII of
the interval; it is trained for 5000 episodes per network size on 30 seeds disjoint from the evaluation seeds and
evaluated deterministically on the same episodes as every other policy. \emph{Nominal SENTINEL} runs Algorithm~1
with the nominal scenario alone ($L=1$). \emph{SENTINEL} is the robust schedule with the five-scenario set. \emph{SENTINEL
+ trigger} uses SENTINEL's association and bandwidth with transmissions triggered by the observed mismatch,
rate-capped at the chosen period. Every policy receives the same acknowledgement feedback and applies the same
link-failure rule of Section~III-F. The exact reference for Section~VI-D is the integer linear program
$\mathscr{P}_3$ solved with HiGHS~\cite{HiGHS2018}.

Fig.~\ref{fig:ue_sweep} and Table~\ref{tab:tle} give the median average AoII from 5 to 20 UEs. Three groups of
policies are visible. The state-aware per-slot policies, the AoII threshold and the Whittle index, are the lowest at

redistributes load as satellites come and go. SENTINEL with the state-aware trigger lies between the two groups at

raises the informative-update ratio from 0.23 to 0.96 while retaining the certificate of the schedule it runs on.
Random scheduling is at 6, and the trained PPO agent matches the nominal schedule at 5 UEs (0.280 against 0.253) and
degrades to 0.446 at 20 UEs as its action space grows; it routes all traffic to satellites and, like SENTINEL, samples
without observing the source, so it never reaches the state-aware group.

The other metrics follow the same pattern. Delivery ratios are 0.92 to 0.94 for every policy except random, and the
per-slot policies deliver with 2.8 to 3.0~ms of end-to-end latency against 4.7 to 5.8~ms for the SENTINEL
schedules, whose dedicated bandwidth levels are smaller than an equal share of an otherwise idle node. The satellite
tier carries 73 to 79\% of robust SENTINEL's deliveries, 77 to 86\% of the nominal schedule's, 97 to 98\% of the
threshold policy's, and all of PPO's. Peak AoII is 100 to 400 for every policy and is set by the 20-slot handover
interruptions and the non-line-of-sight passes rather than by the scheduler. A solve of the 20-UE network used 20 to
58 qubits per domain and the orchestration loop consumed 34 to 223~s of solver time per ten-minute run, counted in
the AoII through $\epsilon_i$.

\subsection{The Certificate and the Value of Robustness}
\label{sec:certificate}
%-----------------------------------------------------------------------

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.78\columnwidth]{figures/certificate_vs_realized.pdf}
\caption{Certified worst-case AoII of each orchestration run against its realized average AoII, robust and nominal
SENTINEL, all network sizes and seeds of Fig.~\ref{fig:ue_sweep}. Points below the diagonal are runs in which the
realized average stayed within the certificate.}
\label{fig:certificate}
\end{figure}

Fig.~\ref{fig:certificate} plots the certified worst case of every run against the AoII it realized. The realized
average stayed within the certificate in 98.7\% of orchestration epochs for the robust schedule and 98.5\% for the
nominal one. The violations are passes that enter the non-line-of-sight state, whose 17 to 25~dB clutter loss
exceeds the 12~dB deep-shadowing scenario; with a synthetic pass model that produces coverage gaps longer than any
scenario window the figure was 98.0\%, and every violation there was such a gap. The certificate is therefore valid
against the scenario set it was computed from, and the paper states its scope that way.

What the scenario set buys is visible in the certificate itself and not in the realized AoII. Across the 30 paired
seeds, the robust schedule's certified worst case is 2 to 7\% below the nominal schedule's (paired-median ratio 0.93
at 5 UEs to 0.98 at 20 UEs), lower in 67 to 100\% of seeds, with Wilcoxon signed-rank $p\le 9\times10^{-5}$ at
every size after Holm correction. The realized AoII of the two schedules is statistically indistinguishable at every
size ($p\ge0.08$), although the robust schedule's mean at 20 UEs is lower, 0.39 against 0.46, because it keeps 21 to
27\% of deliveries on base stations that no satellite disruption can reach. The gap between SENTINEL with the trigger
and the threshold policy, 0.02 to 0.03 in AoII, is small but significant at every size ($p\le0.013$), and PPO is
significantly behind SENTINEL from 10 UEs on.

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/noack_ue_sweep_average_aoii_median.pdf}
\caption{Median average AoII (60 seeds) when acknowledgement feedback and link-failure handling are disabled for
every policy. All policies degrade by an order of magnitude; the per-slot threshold policy loses its advantage.}
\label{fig:noack}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Without failure feedback.} Whether the network can react to a dead link matters more than any scheduling
choice. Fig.~\ref{fig:noack} repeats the sweep with the link-failure rule disabled for every policy, on 60 seeds
because the resulting distributions are heavy-tailed. Median AoII rises by an order of magnitude for all policies,
the certificate holds in 87 to 92\% of epochs, and the ranking changes: the threshold policy, which concentrates 98\%
of its traffic on the best nominal satellite, ties the SENTINEL schedules at 5 and 10 UEs and falls 25 to 30\% behind
SENTINEL with the trigger at 15 and 20 UEs (Hodges-Lehmann differences $-0.77$ and $-0.71$, Holm-corrected
$p=0.023$ and $0.029$, the hybrid lower in 70 to 72\% of seeds), while the SENTINEL schedules keep 18 to 27\% of
deliveries on base stations. Robust and nominal SENTINEL remain indistinguishable in realized AoII in this regime
as well ($p\ge0.41$). We draw the conclusion plainly: the measurable product of the robust scenario set is its lower
certificate, obtained in both regimes, and the measurable value of a scheduled base-station share appears when
feedback is absent.

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.78\columnwidth]{figures/scenario_set_certified_eta.pdf}
\caption{Certified worst-case AoII of the robust schedule at 10 UEs as the scenario list (nominal, mild, deep,
handover of satellite 1, handover of satellite 2, joint) is truncated to its first $L$ members; mean and 95\%
confidence interval over 30 seeds.}
\label{fig:scenario_set}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Scenario-set size.} To see which scenarios matter, the list was extended by a mild 6~dB shadowing scenario and
truncated to $L=1,\ldots,6$ at 10 UEs (Fig.~\ref{fig:scenario_set}). Realized AoII is flat in $L$ (0.265 to 0.278).
The certificate and the base-station hedge move only once the handover of the second shared satellite enters the
set: from $L=4$ to $L=5$ the certified worst case falls from 4.57 to 4.28 and the satellite share from 0.79 to 0.74.
The mild scenario never binds: removing it from the full set leaves the certified worst case of every schedule
unchanged on all 30 seeds at every network size, with realized-AoII differences below $10^{-3}$, which is why the
paper's scenario set has five members. Nominal plus the joint outage alone would reproduce the same schedule at a
third of the dual iterations.

\subsection{Topology, the Whittle Index, and Capacity}
\label{sec:topology}
%-----------------------------------------------------------------------

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/topology_average_aoii_median.pdf}
\caption{Median average AoII at 10 UEs for ground-only, satellite-only, and integrated deployments (30 seeds,
disruption on, logarithmic scale).}
\label{fig:topology}
\end{figure}

Fig.~\ref{fig:topology} compares deployments at 10 UEs. Two base stations cover only the UEs within 5 to 7~km of the
$20\times20$~km$^2$ area, so ground-only monitoring fails for every policy (median AoII 63 to 81). Two satellite
serving slots alone bring the per-slot policies to 0.14 and the SENTINEL schedules to 0.18 to 0.28, and the
integrated $2+2$ and $3+3$ deployments are the best for every policy, with SENTINEL routing 26 to 36\% of its
deliveries to base stations. The one deployment in which the SENTINEL schedules are clearly worse than the per-slot
policies is a single base station with a single satellite slot (medians 1.6 to 1.9 against 0.87 to 0.90, and 5.4 for
the triggered hybrid): with one slot, every natural handover is a total outage for every UE on it, and a periodic or
rate-capped schedule loses more during the recovery than a policy that retransmits the moment the link returns. We
report this as a limitation of scheduled transmission at that topology.

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\columnwidth]{figures/capacity_average_aoii.pdf}
\caption{Median average AoII (IQR over 30 seeds) of the per-slot state-aware policies at 20 UEs as the bandwidth per
node is reduced. The Whittle-index scheduler and the AoII-threshold policy coincide while capacity does not bind
and separate below 5~MHz, where uncoordinated threshold transmissions collapse the shared links.}
\label{fig:capacity}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Whittle index and capacity.} At the bandwidths of Table~\ref{tab:simulation_parameters} a node delivers 19 to
40 simultaneous equal sharers within one slot, so capacity never binds and the Whittle scheduler makes the same
decisions as the threshold policy; their AoII is identical to three decimals at every size and in every topology
($p\ge0.18$), and the threshold baseline of this paper is the Whittle-index policy of this regime. Fig.~\ref{fig:capacity}
reduces the bandwidth per node at 20 UEs. At 5~MHz the threshold policy is 57\% worse than the index scheduler
(0.239 against 0.152, $p=4\times10^{-9}$), and at 2~MHz its simultaneous transmissions collapse every shared link
(median AoII 33.5, delivery ratio 0.16) while the capacity-aware index scheduler stays at 0.16. SENTINEL's dedicated
bandwidth levels avoid this regime by construction. The index scheduler's one weakness is the ground-only
deployment, where its admission rule never attempts a node that cannot deliver at the nominal rate and the
threshold policy's blind attempts occasionally succeed on a fading upswing (81 against 63).

Three observations follow. First, the XY-mixer QAOA returned the exact optimum on every instance whose domains fit the
simulator (8 to 24 qubits), with 100\% of samples one-hot and capacity-feasible; the epigraph encoding, run with the
same classical search, is 38 to 51\% above the optimum on the same instances and needs 133 to 193 qubits, and a

with $\kappa=4$ never changed the exact optimum. The exact reference itself was solved to proven optimality in under a
second on every instance up to 75 UEs and reached its time limit on one in ten instances at 100 UEs.

Device noise breaks the one-hot structure or the capacity of a part of the shots; the decoder discards them. In all
thirteen unmitigated device domains the best surviving sample equals the noiseless optimum, and every decoded network
schedule attains the exact ILP optimum of its instance. The feasible-shot fraction is the honest measure of the
noise: 60 to 86\% at 8 to 18 qubits with up to 139 two-qubit gates, 35 to 43\% at 20 to 24 qubits with about 300
gates, and 2.6\% at 38 qubits with 606 gates, bracketed by the two noise models where both are available. At the
38-qubit scale the device still contains the optimum among 4096 shots, but a few hundred more two-qubit gates would
leave fewer than one feasible shot in a thousand, which places the present limit of the quantum step at about ten
UEs per domain at depth 1. Error suppression does not move this limit. Re-sampling the two largest domains in one
calibration session with dynamical decoupling (XY4 sequence)~\cite{Viola1999} and with dynamical decoupling plus
Pauli twirling of the two-qubit gates and measurements~\cite{Wallman2016} lowered the feasible fraction from the
in-session control values of 25.0\% and 2.4\% to 21.1\% and 0.7\%, and to 13.8\% and 0.2\%, respectively. These
circuits have almost no idle time, so decoupling pulses add gate error without cancelling idle dephasing, and twirling
inserts further single-qubit gates; the best sample equalled the noiseless optimum in every run regardless. The
unmitigated yield of the same circuit also varied by about ten points between calibration sessions (34.8\% against
25.0\%), which is why every device comparison in this paper is made within one session.

What the QAOA layers contribute at this size is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:qaoa_depth}. On every hardware-sized domain
the greedy assignment is already optimal, and the role of the layers is to concentrate a constrained sampler: from
the $W$ state, one layer raises the probability of drawing an optimal assignment from the uniform 0.22 to 0.28 and two
layers to 0.42, independently of the shot count above 128; from the greedy basis state the optimum is retained with
probability 0.99 at depth 1. The optimum is contained in the samples in every run because the one-hot subspace of a
hardware-sized domain has at most a few hundred states. We therefore do not claim a quantum advantage. The instances
on which greedy association is suboptimal, 50 UEs and above in Table~\ref{tab:optimality_gap}, need 140 to 200 qubits
per domain, beyond simulation and beyond the gate budget that the feasible-shot fractions above allow on current
devices; whether a sampler at that size concentrates on the optimal set is the question this work leaves open.
